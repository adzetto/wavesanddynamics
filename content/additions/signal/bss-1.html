Let me make it concrete with an example from structures. Two accelerometers sit on a structure that is shaken by two things at once: a rotating machine, which puts in a harmonic excitation (a 3 Hz fundamental with its 6 Hz and 9 Hz harmonics, its amplitude slowly modulated at 0.4 Hz), and a series of impacts, each of which rings an 11 Hz mode with 8% damping. Each sensor records its own weighted sum of the two sources plus its own measurement noise, x = A s + n: in Figures 10 and 11 the first sensor gets x_1 = s_1 + 0.7 s_2 + n_1 and the second x_2 = 0.45 s_1 + s_2 + n_2, where n is white Gaussian noise. Blind means that we know neither the mixing matrix A nor the sources; all we have are the two records, and we look for an unmixing matrix B such that y = B x gives the sources back. That has no unique answer unless we assume something about the sources, and the usual assumption, used here as well, is that they are statistically independent. Even then, two things can never be recovered: the order of the sources and their scale (the sign included), because swapping the rows of B, or scaling one of them, explains the records just as well. So, in both figures, I pair each estimate with its source and fix its sign before comparing them, and I compare them in two ways: the correlation \rho between the estimate and its true source (1 is perfect), and the signal-to-interference ratio (SIR), the power of the right source in an estimate over the power of the other one, in dB. The two figures separate exactly the same records, so you can compare their numbers directly.

Figure 10 shows the classic method, independent component analysis (ICA) with kurtosis as the measure of non-Gaussianity. It steps through the method by itself (you can click a step to jump to it, or choose the sensor SNR), showing (a) the two signals over time and (b) their joint scatter, one point per sample, with arrows that show where the two source axes go. First the sources: the harmonic is flatter than a Gaussian (its excess kurtosis is -0.26 in this record), the impacts are strongly peaked (1.48), and their joint scatter is the familiar picture of two independent signals, spread along the axes. After the mixing, both sensors see both sources, and the cloud is sheared into a parallelogram whose edges follow the columns of A. The first step of ICA is whitening, z = W x: W comes from the covariance of the records and makes the two channels uncorrelated with unit variance (\operatorname{cov}(z) = I), which turns the parallelogram into a symmetric cloud. But the cloud is still rotated, because uncorrelated is not the same as independent, and second-order statistics cannot see this last rotation. So the second step looks for the rotation y = R(\theta) z that makes the outputs as non-Gaussian as possible (by the central limit theorem, mixtures look more Gaussian than their sources), measured by K(\theta) = |\operatorname{kurt} y_1| + |\operatorname{kurt} y_2| in (c). At the default 10 dB the maximum is at \theta = -30.5 degrees, and the estimates correlate with their sources at 0.841 (harmonic) and 0.830 (impacts), with SIRs of 6.7 and 15.3 dB (this noise record happens to be an unlucky one: over fifty others, the median correlations are 0.859 and 0.864).

Figure 10 also shows what limits the method. ICA undoes the mixing but not the sensor noise, which reaches the estimates through R W, and Gaussian noise dilutes the kurtosis, so the curve in (c) flattens as you lower the SNR (it is drawn on one scale for every SNR): its peak is 1.90 at 40 dB, 1.05 at 10 dB and only 0.40 at 0 dB, where the noise decides the maximum and the SIRs fall to -0.4 and 3.2 dB. Behind this there is a second reason, which a longer record cannot fix: the whitening uses the noisy covariance, A A^\top + \sigma^2 I, which at low SNR mostly describes the noise, so after whitening the two source directions are no longer at right angles, and no rotation can separate both of them (at 0 dB they are 48 degrees apart, which caps the SIR at 8.4 dB). The 4 s record is also short: with only 8 impacts in it, the two sources are not quite independent even within the sample (one of their fourth-order cross-cumulants is -0.30, a fifth of the impacts' kurtosis), so even at 40 dB the SIRs stop at 11.7 and 14.9 dB, whereas 16 s of noise-free records reach 86 and 44 dB.

Figure 11 separates the same records with a method that is built to survive noise: second-order blind identification in the frequency domain (the SOBI family), followed by Wiener denoising. Its default is 0 dB, where the noise is as strong as the signal at the weaker sensor. The key is in the spectrum, (c): white noise spreads evenly over all frequencies and forms a flat floor (dashed), which I estimate as the median of each sensor's spectrum, while the sources live in a few bands that rise above it (shaded: the harmonic lines and the 11 Hz mode). Inside those bands the local SNR is far higher than the overall 0 dB, and the noise of two different sensors does not appear in their cross-spectrum at all. So the whitening uses only noise-free information: the real parts of the cross-spectral matrices of those bands (9 of them at 4 s), with the floor taken off the diagonal. Then comes the second-order idea itself: for independent sources, every band's 2 by 2 matrix is diagonal in source coordinates, so the method looks for the single rotation that diagonalizes all of them at once (joint diagonalization, the principle of SOBI and its frequency-domain variants, which you will also meet in operational modal analysis). Panel (d) shows the off-diagonal energy left after a rotation by \theta; its minimum, at 44.1 degrees, is where the cloud in (b) is turned to.

At 0 dB and 4 s, this separates the sources with SIRs of 32.4 and 19.0 dB, where kurtosis ICA gets -0.4 and 3.2 dB on the same data (the table). And yet the correlations of the separated outputs are only 0.471 and 0.520 (those of ICA are 0.532 and 0.313): undoing the mixing amplifies the noise, so each output carries more noise than signal (output SNRs of -5.6 and -3.9 dB). Separation and noise removal are two different problems, and at low SNR the second one matters most; that is what the last step is for. The noise level on each output is known from the floors and R W, so a Wiener filter keeps the bands where the output rises above its own noise and suppresses the rest (the gains under (c)): the output SNRs go up to 9.9 and 10.7 dB, and the correlations to 0.940 (harmonic) and 0.923 (impacts). If you lower the SNR, you will see the method keep both SIRs above 10 dB down to -15 dB, while the weaker SIR of kurtosis ICA is already under 10 dB at 15 dB, because the noise-corrected whitening and the joint diagonalization only use the parts of the cross-spectra that the noise does not reach. A longer record mostly sharpens the separation (at 0 dB the weaker SIR goes from 19.0 dB at 4 s to 33.9 dB at 16 s), while the waveform after Wiener is limited by the SNR inside the bands: the narrow harmonic lines gain from the finer frequency resolution (correlations of 0.940, 0.967 and 0.981 at 4, 16 and 64 s), and the broad impact band does not (0.923, 0.908 and 0.917). And at high SNR the roles swap: with 64 s at 40 dB, kurtosis ICA separates better (56.2 and 52.6 dB, against 40.1 and 39.2 dB), so the real strength of the second-order method is at low SNR.
