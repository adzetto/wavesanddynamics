Both methods above assume several sources, mixed at the sensors. A very common case in practice is the opposite one: a single signal that reaches two sensors, the second one a little later, as in a pitch-catch test or a wave travelling along a structure past two transducers:

x_1(t) = s(t) + n_1(t), \qquad x_2(t) = s(t - \tau) + n_2(t).

Here ICA has nothing to separate. There is only one source, so there is no mixing matrix to undo, and whitening and rotating two records of the same waveform cannot pull out a second, independent component (the only other thing in the records is the noise, which is Gaussian and different at each sensor). What the two sensors have in common is the signal, delayed by \tau; what they do not have in common is the noise. So the tools change: estimating the delay, aligning and averaging the records, and Wiener filtering, which keeps the frequency bands where the two records agree and suppresses the rest. And when the excitation can be repeated, averaging repeated shots is the strongest tool of all. Figures 12 and 13 are my two animations of this case, recomputed exactly: one signal, 48 ms of delay, about 4 s sampled at 500 Hz, and heavy noise.

Figure 12 works on a single record at each sensor, at an SNR of -10 dB, so the noise power is about ten times the signal's (you can choose the signal and the SNR at the bottom right, and click a stage to jump to it). The delay comes from the generalized cross-correlation in (b): the cross-spectrum of the two records with each frequency weighted by \gamma^2/(1 - \gamma^2), where \gamma^2 is their coherence (dashed in (c)), so bands where the sensors agree count and bands of pure noise hardly do. Its peak, read to the nearest sample, gives \hat{\tau} = 50 ms here, one sample (2 ms) off the true 48 ms. (Over 400 noise draws at this SNR the peak lands exactly on 48 ms in 27% of them and within one sample in 76%, so this draw is a typical one.) Sensor 2 is then advanced by \hat{\tau}, the two records are averaged, and the Wiener gain G(f), built from what the aligned records have in common, keeps the band around the 11 Hz mode and removes the rest. The estimate correlates with the true signal at \rho = 0.855, an SNR of 4.4 dB, which is 14.5 dB above the record it came from (a Wiener filter that knew the true spectra would reach 0.888).

Lower the SNR to see where it breaks. The delay goes first: at -15 dB the highest peak is a side lobe one period of the 11 Hz mode away, at 138 ms (cycle skipping), even though the filter still returns a recognizable waveform (\rho = 0.69); at -20 dB the peak belongs to the noise and the estimate is just filtered noise. With one record per sensor, about -10 dB is the limit for this signal.

In active sensing you can do better: fire the same excitation again and again, with a trigger marking each start, and average. The signal is the same in every shot and the noise is not, so averaging M shots keeps the signal and divides the noise power by M, a gain of 10 \log_{10} M dB (3 dB for every doubling). Figure 13 starts from -20 dB per shot, a noise power one hundred times the signal's, and averages 64 shots, simulated one by one: the measured gain is 18.1 dB, as 10 \log_{10} 64 = 18.1 dB predicts, so the averaged records sit at -1.9 dB. The delay is also refined below one sample, by a parabola through the GCC peak and its two neighbours, which moves it from 48 ms to \hat{\tau} = 48.2 ms.

The table at the bottom left of the figure shows what each number of shots buys at the chosen SNR. With one shot the GCC peak is a noise peak (-101.9 ms) and the estimate is worthless (\rho = 0.00); from 16 shots on, the delay stays within one sample of 48 ms (48.4, 48.2, 48.2 and 48.3 ms for 16, 64, 256 and 1,024 shots) and the correlation climbs from 0.90 to 0.97, 0.99 and 1.00. (This is not luck: over 200 simulated shot sequences at -20 dB, the delay lands within one sample in 4% of single shots, 78% of 16-shot averages and all of the 64-shot ones.) At 64 shots the estimate reaches \rho = 0.967, an SNR of 11.6 dB against -20 dB for one shot. Try -30 dB: one shot fails completely, while a few hundred averaged shots still recover the waveform.
