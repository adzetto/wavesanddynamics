To make all this concrete, Figure 4 takes about the simplest inverse problem I know: undoing a blur. The hidden cause m is a smooth bump and a sharp block on 64 cells, the system G is a Gaussian blur that averages every cell with its neighbours, and what we measure is d = G m + e, the blurred signal plus a little noise (1% of its largest value, unless you pick another level). Going forward, panel (a), is easy and perfectly stable: the blur rounds off the bump and the edges of the block, and that is all. Going back, panel (b), is where the trouble starts. Choose "none" in the figure and you will see the estimate \hat{m} shoot up to about 10^{13}, when the true cause never goes above 1. Now click through the three noise draws: the data change by 2 to 2.5% (you can barely see the dots move in panel (a)), and the estimate changes completely every time. That is Hadamard's third condition failing right in front of you: the solution exists, it is even unique, but it does not depend continuously on the data.

Panel (d), the Picard plot, shows why. Write the system with its singular value decomposition, G = U S V^{\top}, and the naive solution becomes a sum of terms (u_i^{\top} d / s_i)\, v_i, one per singular value. The singular values s_i (black) fall from 0.99 to below 10^{-16}, so the condition number s_1/s_{64} is about 5 \times 10^{16} (so large that double precision cannot even resolve the last few of them). The data coefficients |u_i^{\top} d| (blue) follow them down only until they hit the noise floor, near i = 17 for 1% noise, and then they stay there. From that point on, every term divides pure noise by an ever smaller s_i, and that is where the 10^{13} comes from. Regularization, whatever its flavour, is a filter on these terms, \hat{m} = \sum_i f_i \, (u_i^{\top} d / s_i)\, v_i: truncated SVD keeps the first k terms and drops the rest (f_i is 1 or 0), and Tikhonov rolls them off smoothly, f_i = s_i^2/(s_i^2 + \alpha^2), which is exactly one half where s_i = \alpha. You can watch both filters in the strip under the Picard plot as you move the slider.

The whole game is then choosing the strength. Too much, and the estimate is stable but biased: the bump flattens and the block loses its edges (underfitting, in machine learning language). Too little, and the noisy terms leak back in (overfitting). Press "sweep" and you will see both. Panel (c), the L-curve, plots the size of the solution against how well it fits the data, one point per strength, on log axes; its two arms are exactly these two failure modes, and the sensible region is around the corner. In this example the corner itself (the point of greatest curvature) sits at \alpha = 0.014 and gives a 23% error, a bit too little regularization, which is a known weakness of the L-curve when the solution is smooth. The discrepancy principle asks for something simpler and more physical: do not fit the data better than the noise allows, that is, choose \alpha so that \lVert G\hat{m} - d \rVert = \sigma\sqrt{N}, the expected size of the noise. That is where the L-curve crosses the dashed noise level line: \alpha = 0.087, with a 17.6% error, against 17.4% for the best \alpha of all (0.062), which you could only find if you already knew the answer. Truncated SVD behaves the same way: the discrepancy principle keeps k = 14 terms (18.3% error), and the best choice is k = 17 (16.9%). The rounded edges of the block are the price of stability; what the blur destroyed cannot be recovered, only replaced by an assumption.

Most large inverse problems are not solved with an SVD at all; they are solved by iterating, and Figure 5 does exactly that with the same blurred data. Landweber iteration is plain gradient descent on \lVert G m - d \rVert^2/2, that is, m_k = m_{k-1} + \omega G^{\top}(d - G m_{k-1}) starting from zero, and CGLS is the conjugate gradient version of the same idea. Written with the SVD, Landweber's iterate after k steps is again a filtered sum, with f_i = 1 - (1 - \omega s_i^2)^k, so the iteration count does what \alpha did in Figure 4: the filter opens from the large singular values towards the small ones as k grows (panel (b)). The early iterates are smooth and biased, the late ones fit the noise, and the error against the truth, panel (c), goes down and then up again. This is called semi-convergence. With 1% noise, CGLS reaches its lowest error, 17.2%, after 14 iterations, and Landweber reaches the same 17.2% after 177 (both as good as the best Tikhonov solution, 17.4%); keep going, and after 10^8 Landweber iterations the error is about 20 times the size of the solution itself.

Here is the catch: the residual, panel (d), which is the only thing you can actually see in practice, keeps falling the whole time. So "iterate until it converges" is exactly the wrong rule, since what it converges to is the unregularized solution, and Figure 4 showed you what that looks like. You have to stop early, and the discrepancy principle tells you when: stop at the first iteration whose residual reaches the noise level \sigma\sqrt{N}. That gives k = 7 for CGLS (18.1% error) and k = 32 for Landweber (18.4%), both close to the bottom of a curve you could only draw with the true answer in hand. The two solvers end up equally good; the difference is speed: 14 iterations against 177 at 1% noise, and 29 against 23,588 at 0.1%, which is why conjugate gradients are the workhorse for large problems.

Let me end the examples with an inverse problem from my own field. In structural health monitoring, we usually have a finite element model of the structure and a set of measured modal data (natural frequencies and mode shapes from a vibration test), and we adjust the model's stiffness parameters until its modes match the measured ones. This is called finite element model updating, and it is an inverse problem through and through: the forward model maps the stiffnesses to the frequencies through the eigenvalue problem K \phi = \omega^2 M \phi, so the misfit is a nonlinear function of the unknowns, and we have to search for its minimum iteratively. Figure 6 does this for the smallest case I could think of: a two-storey shear building with floors of 100 t, unknown storey stiffnesses k_1 and k_2, and two measured natural frequencies, 3.193 Hz and 7.766 Hz. (I computed them from k_1 = 120 and k_2 = 80 MN/m and then made the first 0.3% too high and the second 0.4% too low, as a real measurement would be off.) The misfit \Phi is the sum of the squared frequency errors, each divided by its 0.3% uncertainty.

Two equations and two unknowns, so the answer should be unique, right? It is not, and this is Hadamard's second condition failing right in front of you. With equal floor masses, the two frequencies fix only the trace and the determinant of M^{-1} K, that is, k_1 + 2 k_2 and k_1 k_2, and every building (k_1, k_2) has a twin, (2 k_2, k_1/2), with exactly the same two frequencies. That is why the long curved valley in Figure 6 (a) holds two exact fits: (124.8, 76.7) MN/m next to the true building, and its twin at (153.5, 62.4) MN/m, mirrored across the dashed line k_1 = 2 k_2. (Notice also that frequency errors of 0.3% and 0.4% already moved the first fit 4% away from the truth, so the third condition, stability, is not in great shape either.) No solver can fix this, because the data genuinely cannot tell the two buildings apart; what fixes it is more information. The measured first mode shape, the ratio of the two floor amplitudes (2.04 measured, 2.10 for the first fit, 2.81 for the twin), removes the twin altogether: the map keeps a single minimum. A prior pulling toward the design values of 100 MN/m, with a 35% uncertainty, is weaker: it makes the true side the global minimum, but the twin survives as a local one.

The solvers in Figure 6 behave very differently on this landscape. From the default start at (210, 30), Gauss-Newton converges in 6 iterations (7 forward-model runs) and Levenberg-Marquardt in 7 (15 runs), quickly and confidently, on the twin, simply because they started on its side of the dashed line; gradient descent zigzags down the narrow valley for 249 iterations (750 runs) and ends there too. Start them at (40, 200) instead, and Gauss-Newton and Levenberg-Marquardt find the true side while gradient descent still ends at the twin, so even the choice of local solver can change the answer. The particle swarm searches the whole map with 28 particles (1,988 runs) and finds one of the two exact minima, but which one is a coin toss (the true one for 51.6% of 500 random seeds): global search cures local trapping, not non-uniqueness. MCMC sampling does not look for one answer at all; its 16,000 samples pile up in both minima, 49% on the true side, where the exact posterior has exactly half, and that two-peaked picture is the honest answer. With the mode shape added, every solver and every sample goes to the true side; with only the prior, the three local solvers are still trapped at the twin from this start, and 26% of the posterior stays there. (Levenberg-Marquardt's damping, by the way, is itself a Tikhonov regularization of each step, which is one reason it is the workhorse of model updating.)
