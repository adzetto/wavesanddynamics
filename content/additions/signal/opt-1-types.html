Figure 7 shows these characteristics on six small problems, one at a time. You can pick a problem type with the buttons, or just let the figure step through all six on its own. On the left you see the problem itself (its contour lines, or its feasible region) with one method actually solving it, and on the right you see which of the four characteristics above apply to it, (i) to (iv), and which methods are typically used to solve it; "local = global" means that, for that problem, every local minimum is also the global one. Two things are worth watching. First, compare the convex bowl with the function that has four minima: gradient descent reaches the bottom of the bowl from any start, but on the bumpy function it simply settles into whichever valley it starts in, while a swarm of particles, searching with a whole population instead of a single point, finds the deepest one. Second, look at what constraints do: in the linear program the best point sits on a corner of the feasible region, and in the constrained problem the penalty term pushes the solution onto the boundary as its weight grows. The last problem has two objectives at once, so its answer is a whole curve of trade-offs rather than a single point; I will come back to it at the end of this section.
