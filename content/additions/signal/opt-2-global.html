Figure 9 shows all four at work on the same two-dimensional landscape, a shallow bowl with seven valleys, and to keep the comparison fair I gave each of them exactly the same budget: 400 evaluations of the objective function. The Genetic Algorithm (a) breeds each new generation from the fitter members of the current one: two parents, each the winner of a small tournament, produce a child somewhere around them (crossover), the child is now and then nudged at random (mutation), and the best parents are carried over. In Particle Swarm Optimization (b), each particle flies with a velocity that is pulled, at every step, toward the best point it has found itself and toward the best point the whole swarm has found. Simulated Annealing (c) is the odd one out, since it moves a single point rather than a population: it always takes a step downhill, but a step uphill only with probability e^{-\Delta f / T}, so it gets pickier as the temperature T is lowered and finally settles into one valley. Ant Colony Optimization (d), in its continuous form \text{ACO}_{\text{R}}, keeps a ranked archive of the best points found so far and draws each new ant from a Gaussian centered on one of them (the better the rank, the more often), so these Gaussians play the role of the pheromone trails.

The convergence plot (e) shows the best value each method has found against the evaluations used. Here the best starting point lies in the second deepest valley, and all four still reach the global minimum, just at different speeds, with Simulated Annealing, which explores a lot and refines slowly, the last to get there. Over 200 random starts, each of them ends in the global valley in about 90 to 99% of the runs, while a gradient-based local search from a random point manages it only about a third of the time.
